What are these abnormalities?

Soft Exudates (SE): Also known as cotton wool spots, these are areas of nerve fiber layer infarction due to occlusion of precapillary arterioles. They appear as fluffy white patches with irregular edges.
Hard Exudates (HE): Yellow-white deposits of lipid and protein that leak from damaged blood vessels. They often appear in rings or clusters and indicate increased vascular permeability.
Hemorrhages (HEM): Bleeding in the retina that appears as red dots or blotches. They occur when weakened blood vessels rupture and leak blood into the retina.
Microaneurysms (MA): Small, round outpouchings in retinal capillaries that appear as tiny red dots. They are often the earliest clinically visible sign of diabetic retinopathy.

Understanding Diabetic Retinopathy Stages

Diabetic retinopathy progresses through several stages, each with increasing severity and risk to vision:

Grade 0: No Diabetic Retinopathy Mild

No abnormalities detected in the retina. Regular screening is recommended for early detection.

Symptoms: Usually no symptoms; normal vision.

Grade 1: Mild Non-Proliferative Diabetic Retinopathy (NPDR) Mild

Characterized by the presence of microaneurysms only.

Symptoms: Often asymptomatic; some may experience minor vision changes.

Grade 2: Moderate Non-Proliferative Diabetic Retinopathy Moderate

More extensive damage with some retinal hemorrhages, microaneurysms, hard exudates, and cotton wool spots.

Symptoms: May include blurred vision, fluctuating vision, or impaired color vision.

Grade 3: Severe Non-Proliferative Diabetic Retinopathy Severe

More severe blockage in retinal blood vessels leading to areas of the retina being deprived of blood supply.

Symptoms: Blurred vision, dark or empty areas in vision, difficulty with night vision.

Grade 4: Proliferative Diabetic Retinopathy (PDR) Critical

Advanced stage where new, abnormal blood vessels grow on the surface of the retina or optic disc. These vessels can leak and cause severe vision problems.

Symptoms: Severe vision loss, floaters, flashes of light, blindness if left untreated.

Important Notice

This automated analysis should be verified by a healthcare professional. Please consult with an ophthalmologist or retina specialist for a comprehensive examination and personalized treatment plan based on your specific condition.

The severity of diabetic retinopathy determines the urgency of specialist consultation:

  • Mild to Moderate: Regular follow-up within 6-12 months is typically recommended.
  • Severe: Consultation within 1-3 months is advised.
  • Proliferative or with Macular Edema: Urgent referral (within weeks) to a retina specialist is necessary.
